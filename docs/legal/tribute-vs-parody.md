# Tribute, parody and fair use: what protects us

Written for Orb, 2026-09-29. Plain language, not legal advice. Counsel should confirm the borderline items (last section).

## What fair use and parody actually protect

Fair use (a US rule) is a **defence you argue after someone objects**, not a permission you hold in advance. A court looks at four things: why you used the material, what kind of work it is, how much you took, and whether you hurt the original's market. It is decided case by case, and it costs money to argue.

- **Parody** can win when you copy just enough of a specific work to comment on that work. Using the original to joke about something else (satire) gets much weaker protection.
- **Homage and tribute** are not legal categories. They describe your attitude. Attitude can make a rights holder friendlier. It does not make a copy legal.

## Why good faith and being free don't stop a takedown

A rights holder never needs to prove anything to get something removed. They send a notice (a DMCA takedown, in the US) to the host: GitHub for the repo, itch.io or Steam for the game. **The host removes the content first and does not judge fair use.** The rights holder decides whether to send it. We can answer with a counter-notice, and the host usually restores the content after about two weeks unless the rights holder sues. Repeated notices can cost an account. Being free helps a little with the "market harm" question. It does not turn copying into permission. Fan games are taken down all the time. The rights holders here are Japanese, and Japan has no general fair-use rule, so the US defence is not the only law in play.

## Three kinds of protection, one line each

- **Copyright:** protects how something is expressed: characters, art, music, text, code. Not ideas.
- **Trademark:** protects names and logos that tell customers who made something. Not ideas, and it needs confusion.
- **Character design:** a distinctive character (look, costume, silhouette, personality) can be protected as expression, even when the name changes.

## Where we are really parody, and where we borrow

- **Genuine genre parody:** the Tyrant's numbered "revision" joke, which pokes at endless transformation chains, and the sandwich gags, which poke at the victims-become-treats trope. Both mock a trope, and both use our own bodies, machines and mechanisms. So we do not need a fair-use defence at all. Nothing is copied.
- **Borrowed devices, made our own:** carried glowing fragments and their payoff, a merge of two rivals, a blink-teleport, a weak-point core, a hero powering up when a rival commits fully. Each is kept clear by new mechanics and looks (`q3-screen.md`).
- **The rule of thumb:** parody defends copying that comments on the original. We avoid copying, so we are on much firmer ground than any defence.

## The ladder: where "recognisable" stops

1. **Ideas and mechanics** (flight, beams, auras, tiers, destruction). Safe.
2. **Tropes in our own execution** (henchmen first, rival personalities, a long-transformation joke, a weak-point core). Safe.
3. **Common words** (charge, tier, chain). Safe.
4. **Genre devices with our own mechanism and look** (orbs the planet sheds and the hero carries, an inward fold as the payoff, a lap-around merge). Safe inside the rules in `q3-screen.md`. **This is the top of the "recognisable" zone.**
5. **The franchise's own staging** (a pillar of light into a darkening sky, a summoned being, a set of seven marked spheres, hair that turns gold, a two-hand hip beam pose, two rivals fusing with a thrown item). Risky. This is where a takedown or complaint becomes likely.
6. **Copying** (names, catchphrases, designs, music, art, code, fan-game files). Infringement. No realistic defence.

## What counsel would add

A written opinion on the closest items (the orb payoff, the merge), a review of final character designs and key art, trademark clearance for the title, help answering a takedown or counter-notice, advice on the non-US rules, and a view on the risk Orb is willing to carry.
